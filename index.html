Part 1 · Move

Fundamentals of Mobile Robotics & Closed-Loop Control

Weeks 1–6 Junior + Senior Prerequisite: none — the entry point

Before a machine can make intelligent decisions, it must first master physical movement and deterministic execution. This foundational module deconstructs the mobile robot into its three underlying engineering pillars — Electrical Systems, Mechanical Kinematics, and Software Control. Students explore how low-level hardware interfaces convert raw electrical energy into controlled motion, laying the groundwork for closed-loop stability without relying on external perception sensors.

Module 1.1 · Electrical Foundations & Actuation
  • Power distribution & regulation — battery packs and voltage sag, regulators and brownouts, wiring gauges, and computing a current budget for motors + logic.
  • Motor architectures — DC brushed motors (voltage ↔ speed, torque curves), servo actuation (PWM-encoded position), and quadrature encoders (tick counts → distance and speed).
  • H-Bridge driver circuits — transistors as switches, the H-bridge direction truth table, Pulse-Width Modulation (PWM) for speed, and braking vs. coasting.
Module 1.2 · Mechanical Kinematics & Physical Dynamics
  • Differential drive — body speed v = (vL + vR)/2 and turn rate ω = (vR − vL)/W; turn radius, centre of rotation, and pivot turns.
  • Steering vs. holonomic geometries — Ackermann-style steering compared with differential and omnidirectional (holonomic) motion.
  • Chassis dynamics — mass distribution, centre of gravity and tip-over, wheel-ground friction and traction, and why real robots slip.
Module 1.3 · Software Control & Motion Determinism
  • Open-loop vs. closed-loop — why timing-only motion drifts, and how feedback corrects it.
  • Conditional control logic — primitive if/else execution paths for deterministic reactions.
  • Proportional (P) response — error, gain, and the steady-state offset; tuning a P-only speed controller.
  • Full PID control — integral action and windup, derivative damping, and a step-by-step tuning walkthrough for smooth speed regulation and straight-line tracking.
Students will be able to
  • Trace the full chain from battery → driver → motor → motion, and budget power for it
  • Predict a differential-drive robot's path from its two wheel speeds
  • Implement and tune a PID loop that holds a commanded speed or heading
  • Explain why deterministic execution is the foundation every later part stands on

02
Part 2 · Sense

Environmental Perception & Reactive Sensing

Weeks 7–14 Junior + Senior Prerequisite: Part 1 Labs 2 & 5 included in this pack

An autonomous agent cannot navigate an unpredictable environment through timing and dead reckoning alone. Part 2 introduces the physical world into the control loop by adding environmental sensors. Students learn how physical phenomena — light, sound, magnetic fields, and obstacles — are converted into digital telemetry, enabling real-time, event-driven behaviours and obstacle avoidance.

Module 2.1 · Transducers & Signal Acquisition
  • Analog vs. digital signals — continuous phenomena, analog-to-digital conversion (ADC), resolution, and sampling rates.
  • Distance perception — ultrasonic Time-of-Flight (ToF) measurement with the speed-of-sound equation, and Infrared (IR) reflectance proximity sensing.
  • Spatial orientation — Inertial Measurement Units (IMU): accelerometer (gravity → tilt), gyroscope (rotation rate → angle by integration), magnetometer (heading).
Module 2.2 · Sensor Calibration & Noise Reduction
  • Noise & degradation — environmental interference, signal drift, and threshold calibration against ground truth.
  • Filtering techniques — moving averages and low-pass filters for smoothing; hysteresis implementation to prevent erratic oscillation between two states.
  • Sensor fusion — combining a drifting gyro with a noisy accelerometer via the complementary filter (Lab 2's centrepiece).
Module 2.3 · Event-Driven Reactive Control
  • Sensor-in-the-loop control — feeding real-time sensor streams into the PID loops of Part 1.
  • Reactive behaviours — dynamic obstacle avoidance, wall-following algorithms, and cliff detection.
  • Finite State Machines (FSM) — switching cleanly between cruising, stopping, and maneuvering states; designing the transitions as a truth table.
Students will be able to
  • Read a sensor datasheet and wire it correctly to a microcontroller
  • Convert raw readings into physical units — µs → cm, gravity → tilt angles
  • Diagnose noise and drift, then fix them with calibration and filters
  • Design an FSM that reacts to the environment deterministically

03
Part 3 · Connect

Connected Infrastructure, IoT & Smart City Ecosystems

Weeks 15–22 Senior focus (Junior extension) Prerequisite: Part 2 Lab 6 included in this pack

True autonomy operates within a broader, connected environment. Part 3 bridges the gap between isolated edge devices and networked cloud infrastructure. By examining how data travels across local networks and the global web, students transform a standalone robot into an interactive node within a Smart City ecosystem. The AIoScout playfield becomes an active infrastructure layer providing localization, external telemetry, and remote orchestration.

Module 3.1 · Internet Architecture & Network Protocols
  • Client-server models — requests and responses, IP addressing and ports, sockets, DNS, and bandwidth/latency constraints on small devices.
  • Data serialization & transport — JSON as the lingua franca, the HTTP request cycle, and lightweight MQTT publish/subscribe for telemetry.
Module 3.2 · Local-to-Shared Data Streams
  • Shared telemetry — publishing live vehicle status, speed, and battery health to central servers.
  • Real-time dashboards — live sensor visualisation and camera stream passthroughs.
  • Cloud-to-device commands — over-the-air (OTA) parameter updates and remote overrides, with a serious conversation about remote-control safety.
Module 3.3 · Smart Infrastructure & Connected Playfields
  • V2X communication — Vehicle-to-Everything models: V2I, V2V, V2C in the playfield ecosystem.
  • Interactive field signals — smart traffic lights broadcasting their state over network sockets.
  • RFID localization arrays — passive tags embedded in the playfield as "landmarks"; a reader under the car resolves its position to a node (Lab 6's Octopus-card physics).
Students will be able to
  • Explain the client-server model and choose JSON / HTTP / MQTT appropriately
  • Publish live telemetry to a cloud database and render it on a dashboard
  • Receive and safely act on remote commands and OTA parameter changes
  • Use an RFID array as an outdoor-localization layer, and state its limits

Milestone project

The Fleet Dashboard. Every car publishes status and RFID-resolved position to a shared cloud; one dashboard shows the whole fleet live — and a remote operator can start, stop, or retask any car in seconds.

04
Part 4 · Think

Edge AI, Multimodal Perception & Human-Robot Interaction

Weeks 23–32 + capstone Senior pathway Prerequisite: Parts 1–3

Low-level sensors and rigid logic rules quickly reach their limit when faced with the ambiguity of the real world. Part 4 introduces Edge AI and onboard machine learning, giving the vehicle human-like spatial understanding without cloud latency. Students progress from computer-vision pipelines to multimodal interaction, exploring how Large Language Models (LLMs), Text-to-Speech (TTS), and Vision-Language Models bridge the gap between high-level human intent and fully autonomous execution.

Module 4.1 · On-Device Computer Vision & Deep Learning
  • Why learn? — the limits of deterministic code vs. learning-based perception on messy real-world input.
  • Vision pipelines — an image as a matrix: downsampling, colour-space transformations (RGB → HSV), and convolution as a matrix operation.
  • Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) — building and training an image classifier; real-time object detection on edge hardware.
  • Deployment constraints — model quantization, frame rate (FPS) budgets, and low-latency inference on microcontroller-class chips.
Module 4.2 · End-to-End Autonomous Driving
  • Visual lane detection — colour segmentation, perspective, and extracting a steering signal from the image.
  • Signs & pedestrians — dynamic road-sign recognition and pedestrian awareness with the onboard CNN.
  • Fusion into control — feeding AI vision outputs directly into the motor PID loops of Part 1 ("Pixels to Actuators"), with deterministic safety fallbacks.
Module 4.3 · Next-Gen Human-Robot Interaction
  • Natural language understanding — integrating onboard/cloud LLMs for natural command interpretation.
  • Multimodal pipelines — Voice-to-action (Speech → LLM → Action) and visual query answering with Vision-Language Models; TTS for spoken feedback.
  • Intent translation — converting abstract human prompts ("Park safely near the green sign") into deterministic kinetic execution, with prompt design and safety guardrails.
Students will be able to
  • Train, evaluate, and deploy a compact CNN on edge hardware within an FPS budget
  • Turn camera input into steering commands inside a PID control loop
  • Chain speech, an LLM, and structured actions into one reliable pipeline
  • Anticipate failure modes and design fallback behaviours for AI-driven motion

Pathways

Three routes through the curriculum

Junior form · ages 12–15

Move & Sense

Parts 1–2 over one school year (≈ 24 sessions), with concepts introduced on the hardware first. Ends with the Sense & React milestone: line following with obstacle avoidance and crossroad decisions.

Senior form · ages 15–18

The full arc

All four parts (≈ 32 sessions) closing with the capstone. University-caliber material — same lab lineage as HKUST ISDN 2602 — paced for secondary school, with checkpoints and an engineering log throughout.

Team & competition

Mock mission league

An after-school track that replays the capstone as a timed mission on the smart playfield: cloud tasking, RFID checkpoints, sign obedience, and voice commands — scored on reliability, not speed alone.

Pedagogy

How the curriculum teaches

Hands-on first

Concepts start on the car

Each new concept is introduced through something observable on the car — drift shows up in the data before it is explained as integration error. The formal theory follows.

Checkpoint-driven

Demos and committed code

Each lab ends at a checkpoint: demonstrate the behaviour to an instructor, commit the code. Progress is measured in working demos, not quizzes.

Compounding build

A single growing system

The PID loop tuned in week 4 still runs at the capstone — students maintain the same car and codebase across the whole programme.
